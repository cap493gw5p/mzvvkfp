Scrotal Pain? Why Your Varicocele Swells & Medically Approved Next Steps

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Varicocele swelling and scrotal pain occur when faulty valves in the spermatic cord veins cause blood to pool, producing a dull, heavy ache that worsens with standing, prolonged activity, or heat exposure. Left unaddressed, varicoceles can affect sperm quality and fertility. Sudden severe pain, rapid swelling, fever, or nausea, however, signal a possible emergency like testicular torsion and require immediate medical care.

Treatment ranges from watchful waiting with scrotal support and NSAIDs to procedures such as microsurgical varicocelectomy or embolization when pain persists or fertility becomes a concern. Because symptoms can overlap with other urgent conditions, identifying warning signs early is critical.

Explanation

Scrotal pain can be uncomfortable, distracting, and sometimes worrying. One common cause in teens and adult men is a varicocele—an enlargement of the veins inside the scrotum. While many varicoceles are harmless and painless, others can cause swelling, aching, or fertility concerns.

If you've noticed a dull ache, heaviness, or visible swelling in one side of your scrotum (most often the left), here's what may be happening—and what to do next.

What Is a Varicocele?

A varicocele is similar to a varicose vein in the leg. It happens when veins in the scrotum (specifically the pampiniform plexus) become enlarged due to faulty valves. These valves normally help blood flow upward toward the heart. When they don't work well, blood can pool in the veins, causing swelling.

Varicoceles are common:

Affect about 15% of adult men

Occur in up to 35–40% of men evaluated for infertility

Most often develop during puberty

Usually appear on the left side

Many men with a varicocele never need treatment. But when pain or fertility issues develop, it's important to understand why.

Why Does a Varicocele Swell?

Swelling from a varicocele happens because blood collects in the affected veins instead of flowing efficiently out of the scrotum.

Here's why that happens:

Weak or damaged vein valves allow blood to flow backward (reflux).

The left testicular vein drains at a sharper angle into a larger vein, making backflow more likely.

Increased abdominal pressure (heavy lifting, straining) can worsen vein pooling.

Standing for long periods may make symptoms more noticeable.

Over time, the backed-up blood stretches the veins, creating a soft, rope-like mass. Some describe it as feeling like a "bag of worms."

Why Does a Varicocele Cause Pain?

Not all varicoceles cause pain. When they do, it's typically due to:

Increased pressure in the veins

Stretching of surrounding tissues

Increased scrotal temperature

Mild inflammation

The pain is usually:

Dull or aching (not sharp)

When Should You Be Concerned?

Most varicoceles are not dangerous. However, you should seek urgent medical care if you experience:

Sudden, severe scrotal pain

Rapid swelling

Nausea or vomiting with testicular pain

Fever

A firm, painful testicle

These symptoms could point to conditions like testicular torsion, infection, or other emergencies that require immediate treatment.

How Is a Varicocele Diagnosed?

A healthcare provider typically diagnoses a varicocele through:

1. Physical Exam

Examined while standing and possibly during a Valsalva maneuver (bearing down).

The enlarged veins may be visible or felt.

2. Scrotal Ultrasound

Confirms enlarged veins.

Measures blood flow and vein diameter.

Rules out other causes of swelling.

Imaging is especially important if:

The varicocele appears suddenly.

It's only on the right side.

It doesn't shrink when lying down.

In rare cases, additional imaging may be needed to rule out underlying abdominal issues.

Can a Varicocele Affect Fertility?

Yes, it can.

A varicocele is one of the most common correctable causes of male infertility. It may affect fertility by:

Raising testicular temperature

Reducing oxygen supply

Increasing oxidative stress

Impairing sperm production

However, not every man with a varicocele will have fertility problems. Many men father children without treatment.

If you and your partner are trying to conceive and have concerns, a doctor may recommend:

Semen analysis

Hormone testing

Ultrasound imaging

Medically Approved Next Steps

Treatment depends on your symptoms and goals.

✅ If You Have No Pain and No Fertility Issues

You may not need treatment. Monitoring is often enough.

Supportive steps include:

Wearing supportive underwear

Avoiding prolonged standing

Using over-the-counter pain relievers (if approved by your doctor)

Regular follow-ups may be recommended, especially for adolescents.

✅ If You Have Mild Discomfort

Doctors may suggest:

Scrotal support

NSAIDs (such as ibuprofen, if medically appropriate)

Activity modification

If pain persists or interferes with daily life, further evaluation is needed.

✅ If You Have Fertility Concerns or Persistent Pain

Procedural treatment may be recommended.

1. Varicocelectomy (Surgical Repair)

Performed by a urologist.

Ties off the affected veins.

Usually outpatient.

Microsurgical approaches have the lowest complication rates.

2. Percutaneous Embolization

Minimally invasive.

A radiologist blocks the problematic veins using coils or a solution.

What Results Can You Expect After Treatment?

Pain relief in the majority of men treated for discomfort.

Improved sperm parameters in many men treated for infertility.

Gradual improvement (often takes 3–6 months for sperm changes).

No procedure guarantees fertility, but treatment can significantly improve the odds in appropriate cases.

Can a Varicocele Go Away on Its Own?

In most cases, a varicocele does not completely disappear without treatment. However:

Symptoms may remain stable.

Some men never develop complications.

Mild cases may not worsen.

Observation is often safe when guided by a doctor.

The Bottom Line

A varicocele is a common cause of scrotal swelling and aching. It happens when blood pools in enlarged veins due to faulty valves. Most cases are not dangerous, but they can cause discomfort or affect fertility in some men.
