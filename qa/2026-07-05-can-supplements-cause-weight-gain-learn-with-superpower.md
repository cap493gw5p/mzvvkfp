What happens in your body when you take supplements

Supplements don't operate in isolation. When you swallow a capsule or mix a powder into water, you introduce molecules that interact with existing metabolic pathways. Some supplements contain calories. Others shift how your body handles water, glucose, or stored energy. A few influence hormones that regulate appetite or fat storage.

The distinction that matters most: does the supplement add energy (calories), or does it change how your body processes energy? These are fundamentally different problems with different solutions.

Supplements that add calories

Protein powders and mass gainers deliver 100–1,200 calories per serving depending on formulation.

Fish oil and other oil-based supplements contribute 9 calories per gram of fat.

Collagen peptides provide roughly 4 calories per gram of protein.

Supplements that shift water balance

Creatine increases total body water by 2–5 pounds during the loading phase, retained primarily in muscle tissue.

High-sodium electrolyte formulas can cause temporary extracellular fluid retention.

Supplements that influence appetite or metabolism

Ashwagandha may help lower cortisol, which may reduce stress-driven cravings and overeating.

Omega-3 fatty acids may modestly support insulin sensitivity in some people, evidence is strongest in those with insulin resistance or metabolic syndrome, which can influence how the body processes glucose.

Certain B vitamins at high doses have been linked to increased appetite in some people, though the evidence is inconsistent.

How supplements affect metabolism, hydration, and hormones

Weight changes from supplements trace back to three systems: metabolic regulation, fluid balance, and hormonal signaling. Knowing which system a supplement targets tells you whether the weight you're seeing is temporary water, functional muscle, or actual fat accumulation.

Metabolic effects

Protein supplements don't inherently cause fat gain, but they do add calories. Thirty grams of protein powder delivers roughly 120 calories. Add that without adjusting the rest of your diet, and if your energy expenditure stays constant, you're in a caloric surplus. That surplus accumulates as stored energy over weeks, even though protein itself supports muscle repair and satiety.

Mass gainers amplify this effect. Formulated to deliver 1,000+ calories per serving through protein, carbohydrates, and fats, they're designed for people who struggle to eat enough whole food. For someone already meeting caloric needs, adding a mass gainer creates a significant surplus, producing weight gain that includes both muscle (with training) and fat.

Fluid and electrolyte shifts

Electrolyte supplements high in sodium can cause extracellular water retention, the kind that produces temporary puffiness. Sodium regulates fluid balance outside cells, and excess intake signals the kidneys to retain water. This effect resolves when sodium intake normalizes, typically within a few days.

Some supplements modulate hormones that control appetite, fat storage, and metabolism. A 2019 double-blind RCT (n=60) found that ashwagandha reduced morning cortisol by 23% compared to placebo, consistent with adaptogenic modulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. Chronically elevated cortisol promotes visceral fat storage and increases cravings for high-calorie foods. Lowering cortisol may reduce stress-driven overeating, supporting weight management rather than causing gain.

Omega-3 fatty acids from fish oil have been studied for effects on insulin sensitivity. Multiple meta-analyses of RCTs find no significant overall effect on insulin sensitivity in healthy adults; benefits appear most consistent in people who already have insulin resistance or metabolic syndrome. For otherwise healthy individuals, the body composition impact of fish oil is modest. Fish oil adds calories at roughly 9–10 per standard 1 g softgel, so dose still matters.

What the research shows about supplements and weight

The evidence distinguishes clearly between supplements that cause measurable weight changes and those that don't. Most vitamins and minerals contain no calories and produce no direct weight gain. The exceptions involve caloric content, physiological effects on water, or hormonal mechanisms.

Creatine

Creatine supplementation consistently produces weight gain averaging 1–2 kg during the first week of a loading phase, driven by increased total body water retained in muscle tissue. An RCT in the Journal of Athletic Training (n=32) confirmed that creatine increases total body water without altering the proportion between fluid compartments, the gain is water retained in muscle, not fat. Creatine does not increase fat mass. The weight gained is water and, over time, lean muscle mass when combined with resistance training.

Protein powders and mass gainers

Protein supplementation itself doesn't directly cause fat gain, protein has a higher thermic effect and greater satiety than carbohydrates or fat. The problem arises when protein powders or mass gainers stack on top of an already adequate diet without adjusting other food intake. Total calories determine whether you gain or maintain.

Ashwagandha and cortisol

A 2017 double-blind RCT in 52 chronically stressed adults found that 300 mg of ashwagandha root extract (5% withanolides) twice daily for 8 weeks significantly reduced body weight, BMI, and food cravings compared to placebo. The effect appears mediated by cortisol reduction rather than direct metabolic changes.

Vitamins and appetite

Some older research suggested high-dose B vitamins might promote weight gain by increasing appetite, but the evidence is inconsistent. The doses studied were often far above typical supplementation levels. For most people taking standard multivitamins or B-complex supplements, appetite changes are unlikely.

Dosing, timing, and supplement form

How you take a supplement shapes whether it contributes to weight changes. Dose, timing relative to meals, and formulation all affect the outcome. The amounts referenced in this section reflect quantities used in published research; consult a healthcare provider before starting any new supplement, especially if you have existing health conditions or take medications.

A loading phase (20 grams daily for 5–7 days) saturates muscle creatine stores quickly, leading to rapid water weight gain. Skipping the loading phase and using a maintenance dose of 3–5 grams daily achieves the same saturation over 3–4 weeks with less dramatic scale movement. Creatine monohydrate is the most studied form and doesn't require cycling.

Protein supplements

Total daily protein intake matters more than timing for body composition. If your goal is to avoid supplement-related weight gain, account for the calories in your protein powder when planning meals. A 30-gram scoop of whey isolate adds roughly 120 calories. A mass gainer with 1,000+ calories per serving effectively adds a full meal to your day.

Ashwagandha

Studied doses range from 300–600 mg of standardized extract (typically 5% withanolides) taken once or twice daily. Taking ashwagandha with food is generally recommended, withanolides have some lipophilic character, and co-administration with a meal may support absorption while also reducing gastrointestinal discomfort, though well-controlled clinical data on food-mediated absorption enhancement specifically are limited. Effects on cortisol and stress typically emerge after 4–8 weeks of consistent use.

Fish oil

Standard doses of 1–3 grams of combined EPA and DHA contribute 10–30 calories daily, negligible in the context of total daily intake. Mega-dosing (10+ grams) adds over 100 calories. Re-esterified triglyceride (rTG) forms of fish oil absorb considerably better than ethyl ester (EE) forms, a pharmacokinetic study in PLEFA found rTG provides roughly 70% greater EPA+DHA bioavailability than EE relative to natural fish oil, so a lower dose of rTG-form products can often deliver equivalent EPA+DHA effect.

Why the same supplement affects people differently

Two people taking the same supplement at the same dose can experience different weight outcomes. Individual responses depend on baseline physiology, existing deficiencies, and metabolic context.

Baseline hydration and creatine response

People with lower baseline muscle creatine stores, often vegetarians or those with lower muscle mass, experience more pronounced water retention when starting creatine. Someone with already-saturated stores may see minimal weight change from the same dose.

Stress levels and cortisol

Ashwagandha's effects are most noticeable in people with elevated baseline cortisol. If your cortisol sits in a healthy range, ashwagandha is unlikely to produce measurable changes in weight or appetite. Chronic stress, poor sleep, and high training volume all elevate cortisol, making ashwagandha more effective in those contexts.

Caloric needs and protein supplementation

Someone in a caloric deficit can add protein powder without gaining weight, the calories fit within their energy budget. Someone in energy balance or surplus gains weight when they add protein without reducing other food intake. The supplement isn't the problem; total energy intake is.

Insulin sensitivity and omega-3s

People with insulin resistance or metabolic syndrome may see more body composition benefit from omega-3 supplementation than metabolically healthy individuals, meta-analyses of RCTs find meaningful insulin sensitivity improvements are most consistent in this subgroup, with limited effect in otherwise healthy adults. Omega-3s may support insulin signaling, affecting how the body partitions nutrients between storage and oxidation.

Gut absorption and nutrient status

People with compromised gut function, low stomach acid, inflammatory bowel conditions, or dysbiosis, may absorb less of a given supplement, reducing its effects. Conversely, someone with a documented deficiency (low vitamin D, low iron) experiences more pronounced changes when supplementing compared to someone with adequate stores.

Using biomarkers to track supplement effects

The scale tells you weight changed, but not why. Biomarkers provide the context that turns a number on a scale into actionable information. Tracking relevant markers before starting a supplement, and monitoring changes over 8–12 weeks, distinguishes water retention from muscle gain from fat accumulation.

For creatine, tracking creatinine confirms that muscle creatine stores are saturating. An increase in creatinine after starting creatine is expected and doesn't indicate kidney dysfunction in healthy people. Pairing this with body composition analysis shows whether weight gain is lean mass or fat.

For cortisol-related supplements like ashwagandha, measuring morning cortisol and DHEA-S gives you a baseline stress profile. A reduction in cortisol alongside stable or improved DHEA-S suggests the supplement is modulating the HPA axis as intended. If weight decreases alongside cortisol reduction, the mechanism is likely reduced stress-driven eating rather than direct metabolic change.

For protein supplementation, tracking fasting glucose, insulin, and HbA1c over time shows whether increased intake is affecting glucose metabolism. Protein has minimal impact on blood sugar compared to carbohydrates, but a high-calorie mass gainer can shift these markers if it drives a sustained caloric surplus. Aim to understand your optimal fasting insulin before and after adding caloric supplements.

For omega-3 supplementation, measuring triglycerides, HDL cholesterol, and hs-CRP shows whether the supplement is improving metabolic health. Omega-3s may help lower triglycerides and reduce inflammation, both markers associated with healthier body composition over time.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can taking vitamins cause weight gain?

Most vitamins contain no calories and don't directly cause weight gain. Some research suggests high-dose B vitamins might increase appetite in certain people, but this effect is inconsistent and doesn't appear with standard multivitamin doses. If you're gaining weight while taking vitamins, diet, activity, or hormonal changes are more likely responsible.

Does creatine make you gain fat?

No. Creatine increases total body water retained in muscle tissue, not fat. The typical weight increase, 2–5 pounds in the first week of a loading phase, reflects this water accumulation, not adipose tissue. Research shows creatine does not alter the ratio between fluid compartments and does not increase body fat percentage; over time, it supports lean muscle growth when combined with resistance training.

Will protein powder cause weight gain if I'm not working out?

Protein powder causes weight gain if it creates a caloric surplus, more calories consumed than burned. If you're sedentary and add protein powder without reducing other food intake, the extra calories can accumulate as fat. Protein itself doesn't cause fat storage; excess total energy does.

Can ashwagandha help with weight loss?

Ashwagandha may support weight management by helping to lower cortisol, which may reduce stress-related cravings and overeating. Research suggests reductions in body weight and food cravings in chronically stressed people taking 300–600 mg daily. It addresses one specific mechanism, elevated cortisol, rather than acting as a broad weight loss supplement.

How long does creatine water weight last?

Creatine-related water retention stabilizes within 1–3 weeks as muscle creatine stores saturate. The initial weight gain occurs during the loading phase or first few weeks of supplementation. Once saturated, water levels stay elevated but stable. Any further weight gain typically reflects lean muscle mass if you're training consistently.

Do fish oil supplements cause weight gain?

Fish oil contains calories, roughly 9 per gram of fat, so high doses can contribute to a caloric surplus. Standard doses of 1–3 grams of EPA/DHA add 10–30 calories daily, which is negligible. Evidence for fish oil improving insulin sensitivity in healthy adults is mixed; the more consistent findings are reductions in triglycerides and inflammation at higher doses, neither of which promotes fat gain.

Can collagen peptides cause weight gain?

Collagen peptides provide roughly 4 calories per gram of protein and can contribute to a caloric surplus if added on top of an adequate diet without adjusting other intake. A standard 10–20 g serving adds approximately 40–80 calories, and because collagen lacks sufficient leucine to meaningfully stimulate muscle-protein synthesis, surplus calories are more likely stored as fat than muscle without resistance training. For most people at typical doses, the caloric contribution is modest.

Do B vitamins cause weight gain?

B vitamins at standard supplemental doses do not cause weight gain; older research linking high-dose B vitamins to increased appetite used doses far above typical supplementation levels and produced inconsistent results. B vitamins contain no calories and do not directly influence fat storage hormones, and B12, folate, and B-complex at standard doses have no documented body-composition effects in otherwise healthy adults. Evidence is inconsistent even at high doses.
