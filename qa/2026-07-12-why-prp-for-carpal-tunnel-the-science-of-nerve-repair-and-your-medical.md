Why PRP for Carpal Tunnel? The Science of Nerve Repair and Your Medical Next Steps

Carpal Tunnel Syndrome (CTS) affects millions worldwide, causing numbness, tingling, and weakness in the hand. Traditional treatments include wrist splints, activity modification, anti-inflammatory medications, steroid injections, and, in more severe cases, surgery. Platelet-Rich Plasma (PRP) has emerged as an innovative, minimally invasive option for CTS. This article explains why PRP for carpal tunnel is gaining attention, the science of nerve repair behind it, and the steps you can take next.

The Science of Nerve Repair

Nerves have a limited ability to regenerate. Chronic compression—as seen in CTS—can lead to demyelination (loss of the protective sheath) and nerve fiber damage. PRP may help by:

Delivering Growth Factors

Platelet-Derived Growth Factor (PDGF)

Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor (VEGF)

Nerve Growth Factor (NGF)

These factors stimulate Schwann cells (the cells that produce myelin) to repair and remyelinate nerve fibers.

Modulating InflammationChronic inflammation contributes to scarring and fibrosis around the median nerve. PRP's anti-inflammatory cytokines help reduce swelling and limit further damage.

Evidence Behind PRP for Carpal Tunnel

While research is ongoing, several studies have shown promising results:

Small randomized controlled trials reported significant improvements in symptom scores and nerve conduction studies compared to steroid injections.

Cohort studies demonstrated reduced pain, improved grip strength, and higher patient satisfaction at 3- to 6-month follow-ups.

Case series noted few adverse effects, with most patients tolerating PRP injections well.

Key takeaways from the literature:

PRP may offer longer-lasting relief than steroids.

Functional improvements (e.g., grip strength, dexterity) are often reported.

Minimal risk of allergic reaction or systemic side effects, since PRP is autologous (from your own blood).

Who Is a Good Candidate?

PRP for carpal tunnel is best suited for individuals who:

Have mild to moderate CTS symptoms

Have tried conservative measures (splints, NSAIDs) without full relief

Prefer to delay or avoid surgery

Have no active infection or bleeding disorders

PRP may not be appropriate if you have severe nerve damage confirmed by electrodiagnostic testing or significant muscle wasting. In such cases, surgical release remains the gold standard.

What to Expect During a PRP Procedure

Blood DrawA small amount of your blood (20–60 mL) is collected.

CentrifugationThe sample is spun to separate platelets from red and white blood cells.

PreparationThe platelet-rich layer is extracted and sometimes mixed with a small amount of your plasma.

Ultrasound-Guided InjectionA specialist uses ultrasound to precisely inject PRP around the median nerve within the carpal tunnel.

Post-Injection Care

Rest the wrist for 24–48 hours

Apply ice to reduce discomfort

Gradually return to normal activities over 1–2 weeks

Most patients feel mild pressure or aching around the injection site for a day or two. Pain relief and functional gains often develop over 4–12 weeks as the growth factors take effect.

Benefits and Risks

Benefits

Minimally invasive, outpatient procedure

Risks

Temporary pain or bruising at the injection site

Mild swelling lasting 1–2 days

Rare infection (less than 1%)

No guaranteed success—some patients may not respond

Overall, PRP for carpal tunnel is well tolerated. Your provider will discuss specific risks based on your health history.
