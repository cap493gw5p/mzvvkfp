OSCAR LARRAINZAR, A 42-year-old from West Covina, California, thought he just needed surgery to repair his broken nose. His presurgery blood work revealed another problem—impaired kidney function, which quickly led to kidney failure. To complicate matters further, blood in his urine turned out to be from a rare form of bladder cancer. The surgery to treat the cancer left him with a bladder no bigger than a walnut. Over the months to come, more symptoms emerged. “It was painful,” he says.

Additional scans found tumors on his kidneys, both of which had to be removed. For the next seven years, Larrainzar couldn’t get rid of his body’s waste through peeing. Every night, he had to hook himself up to a dialysis machine to clear it out. That wasn’t all that bothered him. He was retaining water. His weight ballooned. He struggled to do things he used to love, like taking his son to Dodgers games. “I was always thinking about whether I wanted to go home, because I just felt tired and couldn’t enjoy the moment,” he says.

Larrainzar was feeling frustrated when his doctors asked him to come in for yet another appointment. There he met Nima Nassiri, MD, an assistant clinical professor of urology at the UCLA David Geffen School of Medicine. Coincidentally, while in training, Dr. Nassiri had scrubbed into Larrainzar’s kidney surgeries. Since then, Dr. Nassiri and Inderbir Gill, MD, chair of the department of urology at USC Keck School of Medicine, had developed a first-of-its-kind bladder transplant technique.

The biggest challenge is step one: retrieving a bladder from a deceased organ donor. “You have to keep all the very delicate and intricate blood vessels that supply the bladder intact,” says Dr. Gill. “No one had attempted it before.”

They perfected the procedure in pigs, then cadavers, then brain-dead patients who’d donated their bodies for research. The doctors believed they could change Larrainzar’s life by giving him a bladder—but he had to have a kidney transplant at the same time. And he couldn’t get just the kidney, because the urine it makes needs somewhere—a bladder—to be stored.

Was Larrainzar willing to be the first to try it? “I felt like I had nothing else to risk,” he says. “If I don’t take this, then probably that’s my last chance to get better and have a better life.”

Last May, Dr. Nassiri and Dr. Gill spent seven hours recovering the donor bladder and eight operating on Larrainzar. First, they connected a donor kidney to his blood vessels. “Sometimes kidneys can take up to a week to kick in, but as soon as we plugged it in, it pinked up beautifully,” says Dr. Nassiri. “It had great blood flow and made urine right away, a good sign.”

Next, they connected the bladder to two major blood vessels and the tiny floor remaining from Larrainzar’s original bladder. But a few weeks after surgery, his wound became infected and required more surgery to wash it out and repair his abdominal wall. He had muscle pain, a rare side effect of one of the antibiotics. Yet by month three, he was stronger, using the bathroom normally, and down 30 pounds.

“What’s most impressive, and what we were hoping for but certainly not expecting, was that he has great sensation,” says Dr. Nassiri. “He can feel when his bladder is full, and he can safely store nearly a third of a liter, a normal amount of urine.” Larrainzar has noticed that he has more energy, and he recently returned to work. “Not being connected to a machine every night makes a huge difference,” he says.

He is living better and could very well live longer than he otherwise would have thanks to the surgery, the doctors say. The average life expectancy for people on dialysis is 5 to 10 years. Larrainzar was already on year seven and running out of options, since his dialysis was starting to fail.
