Doctor's Note: Can Ozempic Cause Kidney Stones? Why Dehydration is Your Enemy

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Ozempic can raise your risk of kidney stones by causing dehydration. Common gastrointestinal side effects—nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and reduced appetite—lower your fluid intake, which concentrates urine and promotes stone formation. To prevent kidney stones while taking Ozempic, drink 2–3 liters of fluid daily, monitor urine color (aim for pale yellow), and adjust your diet to limit sodium and oxalate-rich foods.

Your personal risk profile, medications, and early warning signs like flank pain or blood in urine also matter. Because kidney stone symptoms can overlap with other conditions—and because dehydration effects vary from person to person—it's smart to get a personalized assessment before symptoms escalate. Doctor's Note: Can Ozempic Cause Kidney Stones? Why Dehydration Is Your Enemy

Ozempic (semaglutide) has become a go-to treatment for type 2 diabetes and weight management. As its use grows, patients and providers are asking: Is there a link between Ozempic and kidney stone risk? While Ozempic itself isn't directly known to form crystals in the kidneys, its side effects—particularly dehydration—can increase your chances of developing nephrolithiasis (kidney stones). This guide explains what you need to know, offers practical prevention tips, and encourages you to stay on top of your health.

2. Why Dehydration Raises Kidney Stone Risk

Kidney stones form when minerals and salts crystallize in urine. Concentrated urine—often due to inadequate fluid intake—creates an ideal environment for crystals to grow. Key points:

Concentrated urine has higher levels of calcium, oxalate, uric acid

Low urine volume means minerals can't stay dissolved

High-protein or high-salt diets

Certain medications

Family history of stones

4. What the Research Says

There is no large-scale study proving Ozempic directly causes kidney stones. However:

Case reports exist of acute kidney injury in patients taking GLP-1 agonists, sometimes in conjunction with dehydration.

Clinical trials for semaglutide list dehydration as a known adverse event.

Indirect link: Any medication that promotes gastrointestinal side effects may indirectly increase stone formation by altering hydration status.

In short, the main concern is not Ozempic itself crystallizing in the kidneys, but its side effects tipping the balance toward stone-friendly conditions.

5. Recognizing Your Personal Risk

Not everyone on Ozempic will develop kidney stones. Assess your baseline risk by considering:

Past history of kidney stones

Family history of nephrolithiasis

Dietary habits (high oxalate foods like spinach, rhubarb, nuts)

Chronic conditions (gout, inflammatory bowel disease)

Medications that affect urine pH or volume

If you have multiple risk factors, be extra vigilant about hydration and monitoring.
