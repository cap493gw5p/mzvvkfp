Lupus diagnosis requires a combination of factors, as no single test can confirm the disease. Doctors evaluate symptoms, review personal and family medical history, conduct a physical exam, and order specific blood and urine tests—such as the antinuclear antibody (ANA) test—to identify markers of autoimmune activity. Because lupus symptoms often overlap with other conditions, diagnosis can take time and may involve ruling out other illnesses first.

Explanation

Diagnosing lupus can be tricky because there isn't just one test that can tell if someone has it. Instead, doctors look at a mix of things to figure it out.

First, they check for symptoms like joint pain, skin rashes, and tiredness. They also ask about any other health problems and the patient's family history. If you're experiencing concerning symptoms and want to understand what they might mean, you can take a free AI symptom checker test to help identify possible conditions before your doctor's appointment.

Next, doctors use blood tests to look for certain markers. These tests check for things like antinuclear antibodies (ANA), which are often found in people with lupus. But, having ANA doesn't always mean someone has lupus, so doctors use this information along with other tests.

Finally, doctors might use criteria from medical guidelines to help them decide. These criteria include a list of symptoms and test results that are commonly seen in lupus patients. By putting all this information together, doctors can make a more accurate diagnosis. This way, they can help people get the right treatment to feel better.
