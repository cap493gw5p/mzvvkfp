Internal Medicine

Leg pain while sitting is often caused by pressure on nerves or restricted blood flow from staying in one position too long. Common culprits include poor posture, crossed legs, or prolonged desk work, which can compress the sciatic nerve or reduce circulation. Relief typically comes from adjusting your posture, standing up every 30 minutes, and doing gentle stretches like hip openers or hamstring stretches.

However, persistent or worsening leg pain when sitting can also signal underlying conditions such as sciatica, deep vein thrombosis (DVT), peripheral artery disease, or a herniated disc. Because causes range from minor to serious, understanding your specific symptoms is key to choosing the right next steps. Sometimes, when you sit for extended periods, the way your body is positioned can put extra pressure on nerves or tiny blood vessels in your leg, particularly around the pelvic and gluteal areas. For example, when you sit, the sciatic nerve—which runs from your lower back to your legs—might become compressed, leading to pain or cramps. One reason for this could be vascular compression from varicose veins in the gluteal area that press on nerves, as seen in some cases. In contrast, walking allows your muscles and joints to move, which helps relieve that pressure and improves blood flow, so the pain might disappear when you're active. To ease the discomfort when sitting, try to adjust your position frequently, use supportive cushions, or take short breaks to stand and stretch. Gentle exercises or physical therapy can also help to relieve nerve tension and strengthen supporting muscles.
