Chronic Dry Eyes: Why Over-the-Counter Drops Aren't Enough and What Doctors Prescribe Instead

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Chronic dry eye relief requires more than over-the-counter artificial tears. While OTC drops offer brief comfort, they evaporate quickly, don't address underlying inflammation or meibomian gland dysfunction, and may worsen irritation due to preservatives. Eye care specialists recommend targeted prescription therapies, including anti-inflammatory drops like cyclosporine and lifitegrast, short-term steroids, autologous serum tears, and secretagogues. In-office procedures—such as punctal plugs and meibomian gland expression or thermal pulsation—can provide longer-lasting relief by treating root causes rather than masking symptoms.

Explanation

Chronic dry eyes affect millions of people worldwide. While over-the-counter (OTC) artificial tears can provide quick relief, they often fall short in addressing the underlying causes of persistent dryness. If you've been reaching for drop after drop with little long-term improvement, it's time to explore more comprehensive chronic dry eyes treatment options.

Why OTC Drops Provide Only Temporary Relief

OTC artificial tears are designed to mimic your natural tear film and soothe irritation. However, they have limitations:

Short duration of action: Most drops last 30–60 minutes before evaporating.

Surface lubrication only: They don't target inflammation or meibomian gland dysfunction.

Preservative concerns: Frequent use of preserved drops can further irritate delicate ocular surfaces.

Symptom masking: Drops can hide red flags of more serious eye or systemic conditions.

If you find yourself using drops hourly or waking up with gritty, burning eyes, consider this a sign that you need a deeper, more targeted chronic dry eyes treatment plan.

Understanding Chronic Dry Eyes

Chronic dry eye disease (DED) is a multifactorial condition. Key contributors include:

Inflammation: Immune-mediated damage to the surface of the eye.

Meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD): Blocked or altered oil glands in your eyelids, leading to rapid tear evaporation.

When to Seek Professional Care

If you experience any of the following, contact an eye care specialist:

Persistent burning, stinging, or foreign-body sensation

Excessive redness or discharge

Blurred vision that improves with blinking

Fluctuating vision, especially when reading or using screens

Pain, light sensitivity, or sudden vision changes

Prescription Treatments for Chronic Dry Eyes

Eye doctors have a range of prescription therapies that go beyond lubrication:

1. Anti-Inflammatory Eye Drops

Cyclosporine A (e.g., Restasis®, Cequa®)• Mechanism: Reduces ocular surface inflammation and boosts tear production over weeks to months.• Common side effects: Stinging, redness on instillation.

Lifitegrast 5% (Xiidra®)• Mechanism: Blocks T-cell mediated inflammation.• Onset: Some patients note improvement in 2–4 weeks.• Side effects: Mild taste disturbance, temporary eye irritation.

Topical corticosteroids• Short-term use for acute flares (e.g., loteprednol) to calm severe inflammation.• Must be monitored by a doctor to avoid pressure spikes or cataract risk.

2. Autologous or Allogeneic Serum Tears

What they are: Sterile eye drops made from your own (or donor) blood serum, rich in growth factors and vitamins.

Benefits: Promote healing of the ocular surface, reduce inflammation, and improve tear film stability.

Considerations: Require specialized compounding and refrigeration.

3. Secretagogues

Oral medications (e.g., pilocarpine, cevimeline)• Stimulate tear and saliva production.• Typically reserved for autoimmune conditions like Sjögren's syndrome.• Side effects: Sweating, GI upset, urinary frequency.

Non-Drop Interventions

Eye doctors often combine prescription drops with procedural or device-based therapies to restore healthy tear film function:

1. Punctal Plugs or Cautery

Punctal plugs: Small, dissolvable or silicone devices inserted into tear drainage ducts to conserve your existing tears.

Thermal or laser punctal cautery: Permanent closure of drainage ducts for severe cases.

2. Meibomian Gland Therapies

Thermal pulsation (e.g., LipiFlow®): A device applies heat and gentle pulsation to unclog oil glands.

Intense pulsed light (IPL): Light energy targets abnormal blood vessels and reduces inflammation around eyelids.

Manual expression: Performed in-office to manually clear gland blockages.

3. Moisture Chamber Goggles

Usage: Wear at home or during sleep to create a humid micro-environment around your eyes.

Benefit: Slows tear evaporation and enhances comfort overnight.

Adjunctive Self-Care Strategies

Prescription therapies work best when paired with good eyelid hygiene and lifestyle adjustments:

Warm compresses: Apply a moist, warm cloth for 5–10 minutes daily to soften oils.

Eyelid scrubs: Use gentle, preservative-free cleansers or tea-tree oil wipes to remove debris and bacteria.

Blink training: Consciously blink fully every 20 minutes during screen use.

Environmental tweaks: Use a humidifier, avoid direct air vents, and take regular breaks from digital devices.

Dietary support: Include omega-3 rich foods (e.g., fatty fish, flaxseed) or consider supplements after discussing with your doctor.
