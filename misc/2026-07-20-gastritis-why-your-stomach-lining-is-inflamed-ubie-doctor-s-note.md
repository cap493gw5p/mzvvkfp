What Is Gastritis?

Gastritis refers to inflammation or irritation of the stomach's inner lining (the mucosa). This lining produces mucus and acids essential for digestion, nutrient absorption, and protecting your stomach wall from harsh digestive juices. When this mucosal barrier is disrupted, you may experience discomfort, pain, or other digestive symptoms.

Key points:

Acute gastritis: develops suddenly, often causing sharp pain

Chronic gastritis: builds up over time, with milder or intermittent symptoms

Atrophic gastritis: long-standing inflammation leads to thinning of the stomach lining

Understanding the type and cause of your gastritis is the first step toward effective relief.

Common Causes of Gastritis

Several factors can trigger or worsen gastritis. Often, more than one factor is involved:

Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infectionA spiral-shaped bacterium that lives in the stomach and damages the mucosal barrier.

Frequent use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)Medications like ibuprofen or naproxen can irritate or weaken the stomach lining.

Excessive alcohol consumptionAlcohol interferes with mucus production and increases acid secretion.

StressPhysical stress (major surgery, severe injury) or emotional stress can affect stomach acid balance.

Autoimmune gastritisYour immune system mistakenly attacks stomach cells, reducing protective mucus.

How Is Gastritis Diagnosed?

A healthcare provider uses a combination of medical history, physical exam, and tests to confirm gastritis:

Medical history & examDiscussion of symptoms, medication use, diet, and risk factors.

EndoscopyA thin, flexible tube with a camera (gastroscope) inspects the stomach lining and may take biopsy samples.

Blood testsCheck for anemia, H. pylori antibodies, or autoimmune markers.

Breath testNoninvasive check for H. pylori by measuring tagged carbon dioxide in your breath.

Stool testDetects H. pylori antigens or traces of blood.

Early diagnosis helps tailor the right treatment plan and prevent complications.

Preventing Gastritis

You can reduce your risk of gastritis or flare-ups by adopting healthy habits:

Use NSAIDs sparingly; try alternatives like acetaminophen when possible

Limit alcohol to moderate levels (up to one drink per day for women, two for men)

Quit smoking

Manage stress with regular exercise, adequate sleep, and mindfulness

Eat a balanced diet rich in fruits, vegetables, and whole grains

Wash hands frequently to prevent infections

When to See a Doctor

While mild gastritis often improves with self-care, seek immediate medical attention if you experience:

Severe, persistent abdominal pain

Vomiting blood or material that looks like coffee grounds

Black, tarry stools

Unexplained weight loss or difficulty swallowing

Signs of anemia (weakness, dizziness, paleness)

These may indicate serious complications, such as bleeding ulcers, and require prompt evaluation.

Gastritis is manageable for most people when identified early and treated appropriately. Pay attention to your symptoms, make lifestyle changes, and follow your doctor's advice. If you have any signs of serious illness or if your symptoms worsen, speak to a doctor right away. Always get personalized medical guidance before starting or stopping any treatment.
